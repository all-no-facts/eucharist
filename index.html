A meal, explained

The Eucharist

One table. Four theologies of what actually happens there.

Communion — also called the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper, or the Mass — is a re-enactment of the Last Supper as recorded in the Gospels of Matthew, Mark and Luke. In a Passover meal the night before his crucifixion, Jesus blessed the bread and wine and gave it to his disciples, saying “this is my body” and “this is my blood.” The disciples were instructed to continue the practice when they gathered, and today almost every Christian tradition celebrates communion in some form. Despite the prevalence of the practice, denominations disagree in small and large ways about what the Eucharist really is, what it signifies, how often it should be practiced, and who can participate.

About this page

This page describes each tradition the way its own theologians describe it, without ranking them. Denominational examples are illustrative, not exhaustive — plenty of churches sit between or outside these categories, and individual congregations vary. Built as a reference for comparative reading, not a catechism.
